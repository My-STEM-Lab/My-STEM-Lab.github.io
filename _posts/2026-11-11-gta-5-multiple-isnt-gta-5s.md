*Written 11 November 2026 — eight days before GTA 6 launches. I'll review this afterwards,
whichever way it goes.*

Most market commentary at the moment is about AI: capex, concentration, whether it's a bubble.
I've been watching something smaller and, to me, more interesting — Take-Two Interactive, and
how the market is pricing the launch of GTA 6 on 19 November.

What interests me isn't really the game. It's what the situation tests: **how much weight a
market puts on a single historical precedent.**

## The anchor

Everyone reaches for GTA 5.

The comparison is irresistible. Take-Two closed September 2013 — the month GTA 5 launched — at
around $18. It trades near $235 today. Roughly thirteen times your money, and the obvious story
is that one game did it.

Except thirteen years is a long time, and a lot else happened inside it. That period also
contains Red Dead Redemption 2, NBA 2K turning into a recurring-revenue business rather than an
annual release, and the $12.7bn acquisition of Zynga in 2022. Pulling all of that into a single
number and calling it the GTA 5 effect is the error the analogue invites.

The GTA 5 multiple isn't GTA 5's. It's thirteen years of a company that happens to have
published GTA 5.

## What's different this time

Three things, all confirmed rather than rumoured.

**It ships without a disc.** Boxed copies exist, but what's inside the box is a download code.
Reported reasons include preventing leaks — a real problem for this series — and,
straightforwardly, better margins. Some retailers have gone further than grumbling and said they
won't stock it at all. Sony has separately said it will stop releasing PlayStation games on disc
from 2028, so this is a direction of travel rather than a one-off.

**It's console-only at launch** — PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X|S, no PC version. That isn't
new for Rockstar; GTA 5's PC release came about eighteen months after the console launch. But it
does mean the launch window addresses a smaller market than the eventual total.

**It's $80, against GTA 5's $60.** A third more, for a product you don't, in any meaningful
sense, own.

## The view

I'd expect strong sales. I'd also expect a weaker share-price response than the 2013 comparison
implies.

A 33% price rise is a real constraint on units, and it lands on an install base that has already
swallowed two rounds of hardware price rises — the Xbox Series S is now $599.99, up from $429.99
in late 2025, and the PS5 Pro is $899.99 — as tariffs and the AI-driven memory shortage feed
through to shelf prices. There's no PC market at launch to absorb the difference. And the market
has had a long time to price this in: Take-Two sits around $235 against a 52-week high of $266,
which doesn't look like a stock waiting to be surprised.

## The strongest argument against me

Removing the disc doesn't just annoy people. It removes manufacturing cost and it removes the
retail margin share entirely. Every copy sold is a direct digital sale at a higher price point
than GTA 5 ever charged.

So the unit economics may well improve at exactly the moment goodwill falls. If that's the
dominant effect, I'm wrong — and the backlash I'm treating as a headwind turns out to be noise
around a structurally better business.

## What would prove me wrong

Two things, both checkable:

1. **Launch units track GTA 5's trajectory despite the price.** If the 33% increase doesn't
   suppress volume, my main mechanism fails.
2. **A PC release arrives sooner than Rockstar's eighteen-month precedent.** This has already
   been rumoured. It would pull a whole market forward into the window I've assumed is closed.

I've picked something with a resolution date on purpose. A view you can't be caught out on isn't
worth much — and I'd rather be wrong in public with my reasoning on record than vague and
unfalsifiable.
